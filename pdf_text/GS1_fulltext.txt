Introduction: 
The decline of the Mughal Empire stemmed from weak successors, rising regional powers, and administrative 
collapse  opening the doors for European interference and British dominance. 
Examples: 
• 
Successive rulers post-Aurangzeb failed to control rising powers like the Marathas and Sikhs. 
• 
Regional nawabs of Awadh, Bengal, and Hyderabad began asserting autonomy by mid-18th century. 
Conclusion: 
The Mughal decline didn’t just mark imperial fall it signalled the beginning of a fragmented India, ripe for 
colonial conquest through trade, treaties, and tactics. 
2. Major Battles (Plassey, Buxar, Anglo-Mysore, Anglo-Maratha, etc.) 
Quote: 
“The British won India not by power alone, but by playing Indian politics better than the Indians.” — 
Ramachandra Guha 
Introduction: 
The colonial conquest of India was paved less by sheer warfare and more by strategic alliances and betrayals. 
Key battles like Plassey and Buxar marked turning points in the British economic and political dominance. 
Examples: 
• 
Battle of Plassey (1757): Laid the foundation of British political supremacy after the betrayal of Nawab 
Siraj-ud-Daulah. 
• 
Battle of Buxar (1764): Gave the British Diwani rights of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa the financial base for 
empire-building. 
• 
Anglo-Mysore Wars: Showed fierce resistance by Tipu Sultan, now symbolically remembered in 
Karnataka’s cultural politics. 
• 
Anglo-Maratha Wars: Dismantled the Maratha confederacy; British annexation sealed with the Treaty of 
Bassein. 
Conclusion: 
These battles reshaped India's destiny  not just as wars, but as tactical losses of sovereignty. Understanding 
them informs debates on colonial legacy, federalism, and military diplomacy today. 
 3. Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse 
Quote: 
“Every annexation was a blow to India's unity; every alliance, a nail in its autonomy.” — Bipan Chandra 
Introduction: 
With war-fatigue setting in, the British empire turned to political mechanisms like the Subsidiary Alliance and 
Doctrine of Lapse policies that undermined Indian sovereignty under the guise of diplomacy. 
Examples: 
• 
Subsidiary Alliance (Lord Wellesley): Forced Indian rulers like those of Hyderabad and Awadh to 
maintain British troops and surrender foreign policy. 
• 
Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie): Annexed Jhansi, Satara, and Nagpur, disregarding local succession 
rights fueling resentment that echoed in 1857. 
• 
Rani Lakshmibai’s resistance remains an iconic legacy of anti-imperial defiance tied to this policy. 
Conclusion: 
These ‘non-violent’ policies were strategic tools of conquest that sowed deep political discontent. Their fallout 
helped unite princely states in resistance and continues to echo in India's federal memory.

Examples: 
• 
One District One Product initiative revives crafts once destroyed by colonial trade policies. 
• 
PLI Scheme (Production Linked Incentive) boosts manufacturing to reclaim India's industrial self-
reliance. 
Conclusion: 
The colonial economy weakened local enterprise. Current industrial policies aim to rebuild India’s lost 
productive capacity.
